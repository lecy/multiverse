9. Reconciling the density literature

Within-place evidence compared with the multiverse; cross-sectional studies as descriptive context

Note

Draft, 2026-11-18. The data, models and results of the studies are taken from their full published texts, except where a table marks a study as abstract only.

How this page weighs evidence.

  • Within-place designs carry the weight. These are panels with unit fixed effects, which compare each place with itself over time, much like a difference-in- differences design.
  • Cross-sectional studies are descriptive. They describe where nonprofits are, not what changes their number, so they appear in a separate section and are not used to adjudicate claims.

The mapping of each study onto the multiverse decisions is ours. It is an argument, not a re-estimation of their models.

The question

Local foundations and governments want to know what changes the size of a nonprofit sector. Most density studies cannot answer that, because they compare different places at one point in time, and places differ in ways no model fully captures:

  • history;
  • regional culture;
  • the legacy of earlier institutions.

A within-place design asks a narrower but more useful question: when conditions in a place change, does its nonprofit sector change with them? With unit and year fixed effects, every place serves as its own control, and shocks common to all places are removed.

This page therefore compares within-place results with within-place results:

  • The literature’s panel studies with fixed effects.
  • The multiverse’s within-metro estimates: metro and year fixed effects, 2010–2022, for filers, grassroots and spending.

Cross-sectional studies follow in a descriptive section. They are useful for describing levels and gaps, but not for settling what drives change.

The studies by design

Of the 36 studies reviewed, six estimate within-place models. The rest are cross-sections, pooled cross-sections, or random-effects panels, whose estimates are dominated by differences between places.

Within-place evidence

The within-place studies

Two of the six model flows (foundings and dissolutions) rather than stocks. Marquis et al.’s fixed-effects model covers only very large organizations.

A caveat on identification.

  • Little variation within metros. Slow-moving covariates such as racial composition, education and religious structure change little within a metro over a decade. Within estimates of them rest on small variation and are noisy.
  • Our own recommendation. The multiverse’s design cards therefore treat metro fixed-effects estimates as weakly identified (D3).
  • Better for fast-moving covariates. Within designs are more credible for covariates that move, such as government spending, need and population growth.

That is where this section puts its emphasis.

Variable by variable

Government provision substitutes for nonprofits within places.

  • Three of four within studies. As a place’s own government grows, its nonprofit sector shrinks relative to it:
    • direct state and local spending (Matsunaga & Yamauchi, 2004);
    • government wages (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013);
    • government revenue (Marquis et al., 2013).
  • The multiverse agrees for grassroots. State and local government earnings are negative within metros for grassroots (−.13), and near zero for filers (−.01) and spending (+.01).
  • The exception is entry. Rising government wages go with more new registrations (Mayer, 2025).
    • Those are mostly future grassroots organizations.
    • Combined with the negative grassroots stock in the multiverse, more entry does not translate into a larger stock: new organizations may not survive.

Separating entry from survival is the clearest next test (Part 10).

Government funding to nonprofits complements them.

  • Within the literature. Funding paid to nonprofits rises with the sector in both within studies that measure it (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013; Matsunaga & Yamauchi, 2004).
  • In the multiverse. Medicaid, money paid through providers, is positive within metros for spending (+.08) and near zero for counts.
  • Interpretation. Within places, money flowing to nonprofits expands the size of organizations more than their number.

The literature’s measure of funding is partly an outcome: grants received by the organizations counted. The multiverse’s Medicaid measure is not.

Organizations follow rising need, but needy places also lose organizations.

  • Need rises, the sector grows. Need is positive in three within studies:
    • unemployment (Matsunaga & Yamauchi, 2004);
    • poverty (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013);
    • deprivation, which its authors call tentative evidence (McDonnell et al., 2020).
  • The multiverse agrees for grassroots. Poverty is positive within metros for grassroots (+.08), near zero for filers (−.02), and slightly negative for spending (−.05).
  • Turnover. Economic hardship raises dissolutions (Mayer, 2025). The two fit together: within a place, rising need brings new and small organizations, while hardship shortens the lives of existing ones. The net effect on the filer stock is small.

Heterogeneity does not raise density within places.

  • In the literature:
    • racial heterogeneity is null within states (Matsunaga & Yamauchi, 2004);
    • rising demographic heterogeneity raises dissolutions within counties (Shon & Lu, 2024).
  • In the multiverse: % nonwhite is negative within metros for both filers (−.10) and grassroots (−.08).
  • Interpretation. Government failure theory’s central prediction, that more diverse preferences produce more nonprofits, finds no support in within-place designs.
  • Caveat. Composition changes slowly, so these estimates rest on little variation (see the caveat above).

Density dependence holds. Within counties:

  • higher existing density lowers foundings and raises dissolutions (Mayer, 2025);
  • competition raises dissolutions (Shon & Lu, 2024).

The multiverse’s strongest within-metro term is the population-growth kernel: −.15 for filers and −.19 for grassroots. When a metro grows, its sector lags behind and catches up slowly. Both point to a sector pulled toward a carrying capacity set by its place.

Income diverges, and is thinly tested.

  • Negative within states (Matsunaga & Yamauchi, 2004).
  • Positive within cities (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013).
  • Positive only for elite-oriented organizations within metros (Marquis et al., 2013).

Three studies, three units and three outcomes. No conclusion is warranted.

What within-place tests say about the theories:

  • Interdependence: supported for funding, not for provision. Funding paid to nonprofits complements them. Government’s own provision substitutes. The theory is about the first; tests that use government size measure the second.
  • Government failure: half right. Its substitution prediction holds within places. Its demand-heterogeneity prediction does not: heterogeneity is null or negative within places, in both the literature and the multiverse.
  • Need: supported within places. Within places, organizations follow rising need. That is the result cross-sections cannot show.
  • Ecology: supported wherever tested within places. Density dependence and carrying capacity are the most consistent within-place results.
  • Supply: divided in the literature, positive in the multiverse.
    • The literature’s three within-place tests measure income, and they diverge: negative within states, positive within cities, positive only for elite organizations.
    • In the multiverse, education rises with the sector within metros: % BA +.08 for filers and +.14 for grassroots.
  • Social capital: only the multiverse tests it within places. Turnout rises with the sector within metros: +.07 for filers and +.10 for grassroots.

What the within-place evidence supports

Descriptive context: cross-sectional studies

This section also gives the details of every study reviewed, including the within-place ones.

Most of the literature compares places at one point in time. Those studies cannot say what changes density, but they describe where nonprofits are and how studies have measured them. They are summarized here, without adjudicating their signs.

What they consistently describe:

  • Resources and history. Places with more education, more wealth and a larger inherited sector have denser sectors. History, existing density or region is positive in four of the five studies that include it.
  • Spending gaps. Rural, suburban, high-poverty and predominantly Black counties have less nonprofit human-services spending per resident (Allard & Pelletier, 2023; Shapiro, 2021, 2026). Part of that gap is what size alone predicts for spending (see the size discussion below), and part is underprovision.
  • Identity organizations. Registered and identity-based organizations are more common in diverse places, but community foundations are less common there (Jeong & Cui, 2020; Kim et al., 2022; Wu, 2021).

These are descriptions of levels. They matter to a funder benchmarking a place, but they are not evidence about what an investment would change.

The ten older studies at a glance

Their designs, decision by decision

The studies differ on almost every decision the multiverse varies. Read each column against the decision cards (Part 6):

The multiverse ranks these decisions by how much they move results (Part 8, Findings):

  • The three estimand decisions matter most. Population (D1), identification (D3) and unit (D2) each change 20–42% of verdicts across the 13 covariates: whether a covariate is significantly positive, not significant, or significantly negative.
  • The ten studies differ on all three at once. No two of them share the same population, unit and identification.

So the disagreements are the expected result. Most of the conflicts are not contradictions. They are answers to different questions.

ImportantHow studies handle size: is population in the model?

Corbin (1999) regresses the raw count of nonprofit social-service establishments in 285 metros on its covariates, with no population term on either side.

  • What the outcome measures. Establishment counts scale almost one-for-one with population (Fact 1), so the outcome is mostly city size.
  • What the covariates pick up. Any covariate that grows with size comes out positive. Corbin’s religious diversity is the number of denominations, a count that rises with size. Its bivariate correlation with the outcome is the strongest in the paper (r = .70).
  • The opposite case. Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001) use the same measure, the number of denominations, against a per-capita outcome, and find it negative.

Same measure, opposite signs. The difference is decisions D4 and D6: how the outcome and the size-scaled covariates handle population. The multiverse shows these “avoidable error” decisions (predictor form, DV form, log population on the right) change 13–32% of verdicts.

Recent and related studies

The ten studies above end in 2018. Since then the field has moved in three directions:

  • Density and growth studies now use national data, panels and spatial models.
  • Entry and exit: a separate literature studies which places gain and lose organizations.
  • Location within places: spatial analyses ask where nonprofits sit, and whether that matches need.

The tables below summarize 26 such studies. Unlike the ten, they are not compared coefficient by coefficient. Their outcomes, units and methods differ too much from ours for that. Instead, the discussion that follows asks whether each strand strengthens the claims of this page and the multiverse, or competes with them.

All studies by population, outcome and form

The table classifies every place-level study on this page on three of the multiverse’s decisions:

  • Population counted (D1):
    • Grassroots: registered organizations that do not file a full return. No study isolates them, so the column marks studies of new registrations, which are mostly future grassroots.
    • Filers: organizations filing a Form 990 or 990-EZ, including studies that count employers or establishments.
    • All registered: the full registered stock, which is mostly grassroots.
  • Outcome measured: counts, expenses, revenue or assets. Other outcomes are in the notes.
  • Form (D4): how the outcome handles population.
    • Raw: no population adjustment, or population entered only linearly.
    • Per capita: the outcome divided by population, logged or not, including Poisson models with a population offset. All of these impose a scaling exponent of 1.
    • Log, no log P: a logged outcome with population absent or entered linearly.
    • SAMI or log with log P: a logged outcome with log population on the right, so the exponent is estimated, or the residual from that fit.

✓ = yes; ? = not confirmed from the paper.

Column key:

  • Population: Grass = grassroots (new registrations); Filer = filers; All = all registered.
  • Outcome: Cnt = counts; Exp = expenses; Rev = revenue; Ast = assets.
  • Form: Raw; PC = per capita; Log = log, no log P; SAMI = SAMI or log with log P.

What the table shows:

  • Per capita dominates. Thirteen of the 26 classified studies divide by population, which imposes a scaling exponent of 1. For counts that is close to right at the metro level (Fact 1).
  • Per capita misleads for spending. Spending scales faster than population (β ≈ 1.27 across metros, 1.46 across counties), so spending per resident rises with size by construction. Every spending study here uses per capita or per poor person: Shapiro (2026), Shapiro (2021) and Allard & Pelletier (2023). Their rural and suburban gaps are therefore partly what scaling alone predicts for smaller places (D4). Shapiro (2021) finds the most populous rural counties spend relatively more, which fits that reading. That does not make the gaps unimportant to a funder, but it changes their interpretation: some of the gap is size, and some is underprovision.
  • No study estimates the exponent. None uses a SAMI or log population on the right, except possibly Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen and Kim, Zhang & Liu (not confirmed). The multiverse is the only design here that lets the data decide how density scales.
  • Raw counts remain. Five studies model raw counts with population only as a linear control, or not at all. Corbin is the extreme case.
  • Grassroots are never isolated. Grassroots appear only inside “all registered” or as new registrations. Within places, filers and grassroots respond differently to government and need, so the population counted changes the answer.
  • Assets are almost never the outcome. Only Wu (2021) measures assets, for community foundations. # Do the newer studies strengthen our claims, or compete with them?

The question is asked of the within-place evidence first, then of the descriptive studies.

Within-place studies: mostly strengthen.

  • Carrying capacity and persistence. Density dependence within counties (Mayer, 2025; Shon & Lu, 2024) supports the multiverse’s slow catch-up after growth and the persistence of density (Facts 2 and 3).
  • Need within places. Density rises within an area as it becomes more deprived, tentatively (McDonnell et al., 2020). That matches the grassroots result in the multiverse.
  • Heterogeneity raises turnover, not density. Rising demographic heterogeneity raises dissolutions within counties (Shon & Lu, 2024). The multiverse’s negative within-metro estimate for % nonwhite is consistent with that.
  • Competes: government and entry. Rising government wages go with more new registrations (Mayer, 2025), while three studies and the multiverse’s grassroots stock show substitution. The two can both hold if new organizations do not survive. That remains the main open question.

Descriptive studies: context, and three gaps. The cross-sectional literature adds three things the multiverse does not cover:

  • Spatial spillovers. Strong spillovers between neighboring counties (M. K. Kim et al., 2026; Never & Westberg, 2016). The multiverse treats metros as independent, so a spatial check belongs in its next version.
  • Rural places. Rural counties outside metro and micro areas, where safety nets are thinnest (Shapiro, 2021). They are outside the multiverse’s units.
  • Who is served. Where organizations are based is not always where they serve (Marwell et al., 2026), so density by headquarters can overstate access.

All three are listed in Part 10.

Synthesis

How the multiverse fills the gap

It provides the within-place evidence the literature lacks. Of 36 studies, six estimate within-place models.

  • Their units differ: states, counties, metros and English local authorities.
  • Their outcomes differ: filers, large organizations, foundings and dissolutions.
  • So do their years.

The multiverse adds a within-metro design on one dataset, for all three populations (filers, grassroots and spending) at once, for every defensible measure of each covariate.

It shows which within-place results are robust:

  • Government. Government funding and government provision move in opposite directions within places. The literature’s “complement or substitute” debate is two different variables.
  • Need. Within places, rising need goes with more grassroots organizations, not more filers.
  • Heterogeneity. It does not raise density within places for either population.
  • Persistence. Sectors lag population growth and catch up slowly. Of all covariates, this has the strongest within-metro association.

It is honest about what within designs can show. Slow-moving covariates vary little within metros, so their within estimates are noisy, and the design cards treat metro fixed effects as weakly identified (D3). Fixed effects remove stable differences between places and common shocks. They do not remove changes that coincide with the covariate. Within-place estimates are closer to causal than cross-sections, but they are not causal.

Shortfalls in the existing literature

  1. Too few within-place designs.
    • Most studies are cross-sections, and their signs describe differences between places shaped by long histories.
    • Only six estimate within-place models, and their units, outcomes and periods differ too much to pool.
  2. Within-place designs are short and slow-moving. Panels of five to eight years, with covariates that barely change, leave little variation to estimate from.
  3. The population is unstated or pooled.
    • Studies count registered organizations, filers, large organizations or new registrations, and none isolates grassroots.
    • Within places, filers and grassroots answer to government and need in different ways.
  4. Government is measured as size when the theory concerns funding. Size and funding move in opposite directions within places, so the measure determines the answer.
  5. Measures carry size or the outcome.
    • Counts of groups, and logged totals with population entered linearly, carry size.
    • Grants received by the counted organizations are partly the outcome.
  6. Stocks and flows are not linked. Entry and exit studies, and stock studies, do not speak to each other, so it is unknown whether conditions that bring new organizations also let them survive.
  7. One specification is reported. The decisions that matter most (population, identification and unit) are made silently.

How the literature should proceed

  1. Make within-place designs the default for claims about what changes density. Report cross-sections as description.
  2. Lengthen panels and favor fast-moving covariates. Government spending, funding, need and population growth vary enough within places to estimate. Composition and culture mostly do not.
  3. Use events.
    • Event studies and difference-in-differences around policy changes, plant closures, disasters and hospital conversions.
    • Synthetic controls for single communities.
    These sharpen within-place designs into causal ones.
  4. Name the population. Report filers, grassroots and spending separately (D1).
  5. Separate government funding from government provision, and avoid grants received by the counted organizations as an explanatory variable.
  6. Link stocks and flows. Model entry, survival and crossing the filing floor together (Mayer, 2025; Shon & Lu, 2024). That is how to settle whether rising government brings lasting organizations or short-lived ones.
  7. Handle size explicitly, with log-log models or SAMIs (D4, D6), and use measures that carry neither size nor the outcome.
  8. Report the decisions that matter, in a decision table or a small multiverse.
  9. Build on shared, frozen data (Parts 2–4), so studies differ only in the decisions they mean to test.

For local foundations and governments, within-place evidence supports four statements today:

  • Funding. Public money paid to nonprofits goes with a larger sector, mainly larger organizations.
  • Provision. When government provides more itself, nonprofits do not grow alongside it.
  • Need. Rising need brings new, small organizations, but hardship also shortens their lives.
  • Time. Sectors respond slowly: growth and investment take years to show up in density.

The causal effect of a specific local investment remains unknown. That is the gap the next generation of within-place and event-based studies should close.
