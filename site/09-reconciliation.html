9. Reconciling the density literature

Ten studies read through the multiverse: why their findings differ, and where consensus is emerging

Note

Draft, 2026-11-18. The details of each study come from the full text where it was available, and otherwise from the abstract and from how later papers describe it.

  • Read in full (7): Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, Corbin, Bielefeld, Grønbjerg & Paarlberg, Matsunaga & Yamauchi, Saxton & Benson, Lecy & Van Slyke.
  • Abstract, plus later papers’ descriptions (3): Marquis et al., Kim, Bae & Sohn. Details marked unverified could not be confirmed and should be checked before the page is cited.
  • Interpretation: the mapping of each study onto the multiverse decisions is ours, and it is an argument, not a re-estimation of their models.

The puzzle

Ten frequently cited studies of US nonprofit density test much the same theories with much the same covariates. They disagree on the signs of poverty, diversity, income and government spending. The literature review (Part 1) suggests two explanations:

  • The communities. Places differ in ways the studies capture differently.
  • The designs. The studies made different choices, at each of the decision points the multiverse varies (Part 6).

The multiverse estimates how far each design choice moves each covariate, so it can say which explanation fits each conflict. This page applies it to the ten studies.

The ten studies at a glance

Their designs, decision by decision

The studies differ on almost every decision the multiverse varies. Read each column against the decision cards (Part 6):

The multiverse ranks these decisions by how much they move results (Part 8, Findings):

  • The three estimand decisions matter most. Population (D1), identification (D3) and unit (D2) each change 20–42% of verdicts across the 13 covariates: whether a covariate is significantly positive, not significant, or significantly negative.
  • The ten studies differ on all three at once. No two of them share the same population, unit and identification.

So the disagreements are the expected result. Most of the conflicts are not contradictions. They are answers to different questions.

ImportantOne design choice settles several conflicts: is population in the model?

Corbin (1999) regresses the raw count of nonprofit social-service establishments in 285 metros on its covariates, with no population term on either side.

  • What the outcome measures. Establishment counts scale almost one-for-one with population (Fact 1), so the outcome is mostly city size.
  • What the covariates pick up. Any covariate that grows with size comes out positive. Corbin’s religious diversity is the number of denominations, a count that rises with size. Its bivariate correlation with the outcome is the strongest in the paper (r = .70).
  • The opposite case. Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001) use the same measure, the number of denominations, against a per-capita outcome, and find it negative.

Same measure, opposite signs. The difference is decisions D4 and D6: how the outcome and the size-scaled covariates handle population. The multiverse shows these “avoidable error” decisions (predictor form, DV form, log population on the right) change 13–32% of verdicts.

Covariate by covariate

Poverty: the sign depends on the question

  • The studies:
    • Negative across places:
      • child poverty in Indiana counties (Grønbjerg & Paarlberg, 2001) and in Bae & Sohn (2018);
      • Lecy & Van Slyke’s pooled OLS;
      • Bielefeld (2000): across 9 metros, higher poverty goes with a smaller share of human-service providers (rank correlation −.38 for center-city poverty);
      • Corbin’s bivariate correlation, which he reports as significant and of the unexpected sign.
    • Positive:
      • Lecy & Van Slyke’s fixed-effects models (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013);
      • unemployment, as a need proxy, within states in Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004);
      • Corbin’s regression coefficient (Corbin, 1999), estimated on raw counts with no population term.
  • The multiverse:
    • Poverty is a robust negative covariate for filers across places: median partial r −.17, sign agreement 98%, significant in 86% of specifications.
    • It is weak for grassroots (−.07).
    • Within metros, it is near zero for filers (−.02) and turns positive for grassroots (+.08).
    • At the county level with metro fixed effects it turns positive, because organizations locate where need concentrates inside a market (D2).
  • Reconciliation: the negative and positive findings answer different questions.
    • Across places, poorer communities have fewer nonprofits for their size.
    • Within a market, or as poverty changes over time, organizations follow need.
    • Lecy & Van Slyke’s sign flip between OLS and fixed effects is this distinction inside a single paper. Matsunaga & Yamauchi’s positive unemployment effect is also a within-state estimate.
    • Corbin’s poverty sign also flips: negative alone, positive once size-correlated covariates enter a model of raw counts. That is consistent with size rather than need driving the regression coefficient.

Consensus: yes, once the question is stated. Across places the poverty sign is negative. The positive “need” effect is a location and timing effect, not a level effect.

Diversity: two populations, two signs

  • The studies:
    • Positive:
      • Corbin (1999): number of denominations, strongly positive; racial entropy, positive at p < .05 one-tailed. Both are on raw counts.
      • Bae & Sohn (2018): racial diversity (abstract only).
      • Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, for education.
      • Bielefeld (2000): the most and least heterogeneous of 9 metros have the largest and smallest sectors. The metros in between show no pattern.
    • Negative:
      • Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001): number of denominations, for all, advocacy and mutual-benefit organizations.
      • Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, for social services (Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, 1992).
    • Null: Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004) find racial heterogeneity not significant, with the unexpected sign, within states. Their positive heterogeneity result is for age heterogeneity.
    • Different construct: Kim reports a positive association for income inequality (Kim, 2015).
  • The multiverse:
    • % nonwhite has opposite signs for the two outcome populations: −.07 for filers and +.11 for grassroots. Each sign holds in 83–98% of specifications.
    • Religious fractionalization is a strong grassroots covariate (+.28, positive in 100%) but weak for filers (+.09).
    • Pooling the two populations puts % nonwhite near zero.
    • The measure also matters (D7): fractionalization behaves differently from counts of groups.
  • Reconciliation:
    • Population. Grønbjerg & Paarlberg count registered organizations, which are mostly grassroots. Studies of filers or employment (Lecy & Van Slyke; Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen; probably Bae & Sohn) measure the established sector. The two populations answer to diversity in opposite directions.
    • Measure and size. The number of denominations is a count that grows with population. See the callout above.
      • Against Corbin’s raw-count outcome it is positive by construction.
      • Against Grønbjerg & Paarlberg’s per-capita outcome it is negative, because smaller counties are denser per capita in their data.
      • Neither result isolates diversity (D4, D6). Fractionalization, which does not grow with size, is the measure the multiverse uses.
    • Identification. Matsunaga & Yamauchi’s null race result is a within-state estimate. Racial composition changes slowly, so state fixed effects absorb most of its variation. In the multiverse, % nonwhite also loses its cross-place sign within metros (−.10 for filers, −.08 for grassroots).
    • Subsector. Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen’s opposite signs for education and social services illustrate D11: heterogeneity findings are subsector-specific.

Consensus: emerging but conditional. Preference heterogeneity, measured as fractionalization, goes with more grassroots organizations. Its relationship with the established, filing sector is weak and can be negative. Diversity claims should name the population and the measure.

Income and education: resources matter

  • The studies:
    • Income positive across places:
      • Bielefeld (2000): rank correlation of wealth with filers per 10,000, .63;
      • Saxton & Benson (2005);
      • Lecy & Van Slyke (2013);
      • Corbin (1999): significant on raw counts, but in his words of “negligible” magnitude.
    • Income negative:
      • within states, in Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004): a 1% rise in income per capita goes with about 0.7% fewer filers per capita;
      • Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen (1992), where the outcome is the nonprofit share of employment, not its size.
    • Education is positive in Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001) and in Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen outside health.
  • The multiverse:
    • % with a BA is the most robust covariate in the data. For filers it has a median r of .32, the same sign in 100% of specifications, and is significant in 100%. For spending it is .24, and for grassroots .15.
    • Foundation assets are positive for filers (.11, 86% agreement).
  • Reconciliation: the negative results answer different questions.
    • Share, not density. Richer places can have more nonprofits and a smaller nonprofit share of employment, if the for-profit sector grows faster.
    • Within, not across. Matsunaga & Yamauchi ask what happens as a state’s income rises, holding the state fixed. In the multiverse, % BA also shrinks within metros, from .32 to .08 for filers. Resource advantages show up across places, and they build slowly.

Consensus: yes. Education, and to a lesser degree wealth, go with denser nonprofit sectors under nearly every design. It is the most stable finding in both the literature and the multiverse.

Government: funding complements, provision may substitute

  • The studies:
    • Money paid to nonprofits, positive:
      • government grants in Lecy & Van Slyke (2013);
      • federal spending in Saxton & Benson (2005);
      • federal and local funding in Bae & Sohn (2018);
      • “public subsidies” in Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004). Their measure is total public support from the 990s, which, as they note, includes individual giving.
      • State welfare generosity (AFDC share) correlates .53 with sector size across 9 metros (Bielefeld, 2000).
    • Government’s own provision, negative (a substitute):
      • state and local direct spending, as a share of gross state product, within states in Matsunaga & Yamauchi: a 1% rise goes with about 0.22% fewer filers per capita;
      • government wages in Lecy & Van Slyke.
    • Null: federal funding in Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001). Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen find mostly no displacement (Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, 1992).
  • The multiverse:
    • Direct government (state and local government earnings) is modestly positive across places for filers: .09, 82% agreement.
    • Within metros it is near zero for filers (−.01) and negative for grassroots (−.13).
    • Indirect government (Medicaid, money paid through providers) is weak and fragile for filers: .03, 59% agreement. It is somewhat stronger for spending: .05 across metros and .08 within them.
    • Government grants received by nonprofits were left out of the panels, because they are mechanically tied to the outcome: more organizations receive more grants.
  • Reconciliation:
    • Measurement. The strongest “complement” findings measure grants received by the nonprofits being counted. That variable is partly an outcome, and on its own it cannot separate cause from accounting.
    • Identification. The substitution findings are within-place estimates:
      • Matsunaga & Yamauchi’s direct-spending result, from state fixed effects;
      • Lecy & Van Slyke’s government wages (whether pooled, fixed effects or both is unverified).
      The multiverse shows the same pattern for direct government. Across metros, larger state and local payrolls go with slightly more filers (+.09). Within metros the association is zero for filers (−.01) and negative for grassroots (−.13). Places with big governments have big nonprofit sectors, but as a government grows, it does more itself.
    • Delivery channel. What complements the sector is money paid to providers, not government size as such. That is also the measure most likely to be partly an outcome.

Consensus: emerging, with a distinction. - Funding: government money that flows to nonprofits goes with larger sectors. - Provision: within places, government’s own provision tends to substitute for them.

Whether public funding causes sector growth is unresolved, and it is the open question most relevant to public funders (Part 10).

Religion, civic life and social capital

  • The studies:
    • Church density or religious cohesion is positive in Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen (1992) and in Corbin (1999)’s regression. Corbin’s church density is negative in the bivariate correlation and positive only in the model of raw counts.
    • Political engagement is positive in Saxton & Benson (2005) and
    • Trust is negative in Saxton & Benson.
    • Associations or membership groups are null in Bae & Sohn (2018) and Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen.
  • The multiverse:
    • Turnout is a strong grassroots covariate (+.20, 100% agreement) and modest for filers (+.08).
    • It survives within metros (+.07 for filers, +.10 for grassroots).
    • Social-capital indexes that include association counts partly count the outcome.
  • Reconciliation:
    • Civic participation predicts the grassroots sector most clearly. Saxton & Benson study foundings, the flow most like grassroots, and find engagement positive.
    • Null association measures are expected when the index overlaps the outcome.

Consensus: yes for participation (turnout, engagement) and grassroots organizations. Generalized trust and association counts do not add.

Population and history

  • The studies:
    • Smaller counties are denser per capita in Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001).
    • Counts rise at a decreasing rate in Lecy & Van Slyke (2013), with population and its square as controls.
    • Existing density predicts foundings in Saxton & Benson (2005).
  • The multiverse:
    • Counts scale with population with an exponent of about 1.00 at the metro level (Fact 1), so per-capita counts are a fair metro yardstick. County-level per-capita densities are distorted by small denominators and group exemptions.
    • The pre-1970 endowment, the sector’s inherited size, is among the strongest covariates: .21 for filers and .19 for grassroots.
    • Density is nearly frozen over decades (Fact 2).
  • Reconciliation:
    • History. Saxton & Benson’s positive existing-density term is the same persistence the endowment captures. Models without history attribute part of the inherited level to whatever covariates correlate with it (D8). That includes region-linked ones such as diversity and income.
    • Size. The apparent density advantage of small counties is partly a feature of county units and per-capita measures (D2, D4).

Is consensus emerging?

Read through the multiverse, the literature agrees more than its signs suggest:

Covariate Consensus Condition
Education and wealth Positive Holds across designs
Poverty Negative across places Positive only within markets or over time (D2, D3)
Diversity Positive for grassroots; weak or negative for filers Name the population (D1) and the measure (D7); never use a count of groups (D4, D6)
Civic participation Positive, mainly grassroots Use participation, not association counts
Government Funding complements; direct provision substitutes within places Causal direction unresolved; money received is partly an outcome
Size Population must be in the model A raw count without population measures city size (D4, D6)
History Strong persistence Control for it when the question is current conditions (D8)

What is not settled is causality. Every study here, and the multiverse itself, describes associations. The next step is designs that use changes a community or government can make (Part 10).

What it means for funders and governments. A local foundation reading this literature should:

  • give weight to findings that are stable across designs (education, civic participation, history);
  • treat diversity and poverty results as specific to the population and the question asked;
  • not read government-grant associations as evidence that public money builds sectors. They are consistent with that, but cannot show it.
