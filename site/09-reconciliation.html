9. Reconciling the density literature

Ten studies read through the multiverse, and 26 recent and related studies weighed against it

Note

Draft, 2026-11-18. The data, models and results of all ten studies are taken from their full published texts.

The mapping of each study onto the multiverse decisions is ours. It is an argument, not a re-estimation of their models.

The puzzle

Ten frequently cited studies of US nonprofit density test much the same theories with much the same covariates. They disagree on the signs of poverty, diversity, income and government spending. The literature review (Part 1) suggests two explanations:

  • The communities. Places differ in ways the studies capture differently.
  • The designs. The studies made different choices, at each of the decision points the multiverse varies (Part 6).

The multiverse estimates how far each design choice moves each covariate, so it can say which explanation fits each conflict. This page applies it to the ten studies.

The ten studies at a glance

Their designs, decision by decision

The studies differ on almost every decision the multiverse varies. Read each column against the decision cards (Part 6):

The multiverse ranks these decisions by how much they move results (Part 8, Findings):

  • The three estimand decisions matter most. Population (D1), identification (D3) and unit (D2) each change 20–42% of verdicts across the 13 covariates: whether a covariate is significantly positive, not significant, or significantly negative.
  • The ten studies differ on all three at once. No two of them share the same population, unit and identification.

So the disagreements are the expected result. Most of the conflicts are not contradictions. They are answers to different questions.

ImportantOne design choice settles several conflicts: is population in the model?

Corbin (1999) regresses the raw count of nonprofit social-service establishments in 285 metros on its covariates, with no population term on either side.

  • What the outcome measures. Establishment counts scale almost one-for-one with population (Fact 1), so the outcome is mostly city size.
  • What the covariates pick up. Any covariate that grows with size comes out positive. Corbin’s religious diversity is the number of denominations, a count that rises with size. Its bivariate correlation with the outcome is the strongest in the paper (r = .70).
  • The opposite case. Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001) use the same measure, the number of denominations, against a per-capita outcome, and find it negative.

Same measure, opposite signs. The difference is decisions D4 and D6: how the outcome and the size-scaled covariates handle population. The multiverse shows these “avoidable error” decisions (predictor form, DV form, log population on the right) change 13–32% of verdicts.

Covariate by covariate

Poverty: the sign depends on the question

  • The studies:
    • Negative across places:
      • child poverty in Indiana counties, both for organizations (Grønbjerg & Paarlberg, 2001) and for nonprofit employment, 2001–2009 (Bae & Sohn, 2018);
      • Lecy & Van Slyke’s pooled OLS;
      • Bielefeld (2000): across 9 metros, higher poverty goes with a smaller share of human-service providers (rank correlation −.38 for center-city poverty);
      • Corbin’s bivariate correlation, which he reports as significant and of the unexpected sign.
    • Null: in Kim (2015), poverty is not significant once median income and the other controls enter (registered charities, 3,036 counties). Unemployment is negative.
    • Positive:
      • Lecy & Van Slyke’s fixed-effects models (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013);
      • unemployment, as a need proxy, within states in Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004);
      • Corbin’s regression coefficient (Corbin, 1999), estimated on raw counts with no population term.
  • The multiverse:
    • Poverty is a robust negative covariate for filers across places: median partial r −.17, sign agreement 98%, significant in 86% of specifications.
    • It is weak for grassroots (−.07).
    • Within metros, it is near zero for filers (−.02) and turns positive for grassroots (+.08).
    • At the county level with metro fixed effects it turns positive, because organizations locate where need concentrates inside a market (D2).
  • Reconciliation: the negative and positive findings answer different questions.
    • Across places, poorer communities have fewer nonprofits for their size.
    • Within a market, or as poverty changes over time, organizations follow need.
    • Lecy & Van Slyke’s sign flip between OLS and fixed effects is this distinction inside a single paper. Matsunaga & Yamauchi’s positive unemployment effect is also a within-state estimate.
    • Corbin’s poverty sign also flips: negative alone, positive once size-correlated covariates enter a model of raw counts. That is consistent with size rather than need driving the regression coefficient.
    • Population. Kim’s null poverty result is for registered charities, mostly grassroots. In the multiverse, poverty is also weak for grassroots (−.07, significant in 45%), while it is strong for filers.

Consensus: yes, once the question is stated. Across places the poverty sign is negative. The positive “need” effect is a location and timing effect, not a level effect.

Diversity: two populations, two signs

  • The studies:
    • Positive:
      • Corbin (1999): number of denominations, strongly positive; racial entropy, positive at p < .05 one-tailed. Both are on raw counts.
      • Bae & Sohn (2018): racial diversity (Blau index) positive in all five models of nonprofit employment in Indiana counties.
      • Kim (2015): positive alone, but not significant once the controls enter. In five subsectors it is negative: higher education, hospitals, health, international and public benefit.
      • Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, for education.
      • Bielefeld (2000): the most and least heterogeneous of 9 metros have the largest and smallest sectors. The metros in between show no pattern.
    • Negative:
      • Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001): number of denominations, for all, advocacy and mutual-benefit organizations.
      • Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, for social services (Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, 1992).
    • Null: Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004) find racial heterogeneity not significant, with the unexpected sign, within states. Their positive heterogeneity result is for age heterogeneity.
    • Different construct: income inequality (the Gini) is Kim’s main result: positive for the whole sector and for 6 of 10 subsectors, and it survives the control for median income (Kim, 2015).
  • The multiverse:
    • % nonwhite has opposite signs for the two outcome populations: −.07 for filers and +.11 for grassroots. Each sign holds in 83–98% of specifications.
    • Religious fractionalization is a strong grassroots covariate (+.28, positive in 100%) but weak for filers (+.09).
    • Pooling the two populations puts % nonwhite near zero.
    • The measure also matters (D7): fractionalization behaves differently from counts of groups.
    • The metro Gini is modestly positive for every population: +.08 for filers, +.07 for grassroots, +.08 for spending, with 91–94% sign agreement. That matches Kim.
  • Reconciliation:
    • Population. Grønbjerg & Paarlberg and Kim count registered organizations, which are mostly grassroots. Studies of filers or employment (Lecy & Van Slyke; Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen; Bae & Sohn) measure the established sector. The two populations answer to diversity in opposite directions, and pooling them gives the nulls.
    • Measure and size. The number of denominations is a count that grows with population. See the callout above.
      • Against Corbin’s raw-count outcome it is positive by construction.
      • Against Grønbjerg & Paarlberg’s per-capita outcome it is negative, because smaller counties are denser per capita in their data.
      • Neither result isolates diversity (D4, D6). Fractionalization, which does not grow with size, is the measure the multiverse uses.
    • Identification. Matsunaga & Yamauchi’s null race result is a within-state estimate. Racial composition changes slowly, so state fixed effects absorb most of its variation. In the multiverse, the grassroots association with % nonwhite reverses within metros, from +.11 to −.08.
    • Subsector. Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen’s opposite signs for education and social services illustrate D11: heterogeneity findings are subsector-specific.

Consensus: emerging but conditional. - Racial and religious heterogeneity. Measured as fractionalization, it goes with more grassroots organizations. Its relationship with the established, filing sector is weak and can be negative. Diversity claims should name the population and the measure. - Income inequality. It is modestly positive in both Kim and the multiverse, the most consistent of the heterogeneity measures.

Income and education: resources matter

  • The studies:
    • Income positive across places:
      • Bielefeld (2000): rank correlation of wealth with filers per 10,000, .63;
      • Saxton & Benson (2005);
      • Lecy & Van Slyke (2013);
      • Corbin (1999): significant on raw counts, but in his words of “negligible” magnitude.
    • Income null: median household income, controlling for inequality and poverty, in Kim (2015).
    • Income negative:
      • within states, in Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004): a 1% rise in income per capita goes with about 0.7% fewer filers per capita;
      • Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen (1992), where the outcome is the nonprofit share of employment, not its size.
    • Education is positive in Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001) and in Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen outside health.
  • The multiverse:
    • % with a BA is the most robust covariate in the data. For filers it has a median r of .32, the same sign in 100% of specifications, and is significant in 100%. For spending it is .24, and for grassroots .15.
    • Foundation assets are positive for filers (.11, 86% agreement).
  • Reconciliation: the negative results answer different questions.
    • Share, not density. Richer places can have more nonprofits and a smaller nonprofit share of employment, if the for-profit sector grows faster.
    • Within, not across. Matsunaga & Yamauchi ask what happens as a state’s income rises, holding the state fixed. In the multiverse, % BA also shrinks within metros, from .32 to .08 for filers. Resource advantages show up across places, and they build slowly.

Consensus: yes. Education, and to a lesser degree wealth, go with denser nonprofit sectors under nearly every design. It is the most stable finding in both the literature and the multiverse.

Government: funding complements, provision may substitute

  • The studies:
    • Money paid to nonprofits, positive:
      • government grants in Lecy & Van Slyke (2013);
      • federal spending in Saxton & Benson (2005);
      • federal grants and contracts, and county library spending, in Bae & Sohn (2018). Both enter as logged dollar totals, with population only as a linear control.
      • “public subsidies” in Matsunaga & Yamauchi (2004). Their measure is total public support from the 990s, which, as they note, includes individual giving.
      • State welfare generosity (AFDC share) correlates .53 with sector size across 9 metros (Bielefeld, 2000).
    • Government’s own provision, negative (a substitute):
      • state and local direct spending, as a share of gross state product, within states in Matsunaga & Yamauchi: a 1% rise goes with about 0.22% fewer filers per capita;
      • government wages in Lecy & Van Slyke;
      • total government revenue in Marquis et al.’s fixed-effects models (Marquis et al., 2013). Within a metro, rising government revenue goes with fewer large elite-oriented and social-welfare nonprofits.
    • Government’s size, positive across places: total government wages per capita in Kim (2015), at p < .10 in the full model. It is strongest for hospitals, higher education, international and public-benefit organizations.
    • Null: federal funding in Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001). Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen find mostly no displacement (Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, 1992).
  • The multiverse:
    • Direct government (state and local government earnings) is modestly positive across places for filers: .09, 82% agreement.
    • Within metros it is near zero for filers (−.01) and negative for grassroots (−.13).
    • Indirect government (Medicaid, money paid through providers) is weak and fragile for filers: .03, 59% agreement. It is somewhat stronger for spending: .05 across metros and .08 within them.
    • Government grants received by nonprofits were left out of the panels, because they are mechanically tied to the outcome: more organizations receive more grants.
  • Reconciliation:
    • Measurement. The strongest “complement” findings measure grants received by the nonprofits being counted. That variable is partly an outcome, and on its own it cannot separate cause from accounting.
    • Size (our interpretation). Bae & Sohn’s funding variables are logged dollar totals, set against logged total employment, with population entering only linearly. Federal dollars and library budgets grow with county size, and a linear population term does not remove size from a log-log model. Part of their positive coefficients is likely size (D6).
    • Identification. The substitution findings are within-place estimates. That includes Marquis et al.’s fixed-effects government revenue result, and:
      • Matsunaga & Yamauchi’s direct-spending result, from state fixed effects;
      • Lecy & Van Slyke’s government wages, which are negative and significant in both of their city fixed-effects models, and largest there.
      The cross-place estimate goes the other way: Kim finds government wages per capita weakly positive across counties. The multiverse shows both patterns for direct government. Across metros, larger state and local payrolls go with slightly more filers (+.09). Within metros the association is zero for filers (−.01) and negative for grassroots (−.13). Places with big governments have big nonprofit sectors, but as a government grows, it does more itself.
    • Delivery channel. What complements the sector is money paid to providers, not government size as such. That is also the measure most likely to be partly an outcome.

Consensus: emerging, with a distinction. - Funding: government money that flows to nonprofits goes with larger sectors. - Provision: within places, government’s own provision tends to substitute for them.

Whether public funding causes sector growth is unresolved, and it is the open question most relevant to public funders (Part 10).

Religion, civic life and social capital

  • The studies:
    • Church density or religious cohesion is positive in Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen (1992) and in Corbin (1999)’s regression. Corbin’s church density is negative in the bivariate correlation and positive only in the model of raw counts.
    • Political engagement is positive in Saxton & Benson (2005). In Kim (2015), presidential turnout is positive and highly significant for the whole sector and for every subsector.
    • Trust is negative in Saxton & Benson.
    • Associations or membership groups are null in Bae & Sohn (2018) and Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen.
  • The multiverse:
    • Turnout is a strong grassroots covariate (+.20, 100% agreement) and modest for filers (+.08).
    • It survives within metros (+.07 for filers, +.10 for grassroots).
    • Social-capital indexes that include association counts partly count the outcome.
  • Reconciliation:
    • Civic participation predicts the grassroots sector most clearly. Saxton & Benson study foundings, the flow most like grassroots, and find engagement positive. Kim’s outcome is registered charities, mostly grassroots, and turnout is her most consistent result. That matches the multiverse’s strongest grassroots covariate.
    • Null association measures are expected when the index overlaps the outcome.

Consensus: yes for participation (turnout, engagement) and grassroots organizations. Generalized trust and association counts do not add.

Population and history

  • The studies:
    • Smaller counties are denser per capita in Grønbjerg & Paarlberg (2001).
    • Counts rise at a decreasing rate in Lecy & Van Slyke (2013), with population and its square as controls.
    • Existing density predicts foundings in Saxton & Benson (2005).
    • In Marquis et al. (2013), large nonprofits grow faster in two kinds of metro: those in the Northeast, and those with an early-established business community (firms incorporated by 1905). Both are strongly positive.
  • The multiverse:
    • Counts scale with population with an exponent of about 1.00 at the metro level (Fact 1), so per-capita counts are a fair metro yardstick. County-level per-capita densities are distorted by small denominators and group exemptions.
    • The pre-1970 endowment, the sector’s inherited size, is among the strongest covariates: .21 for filers and .19 for grassroots.
    • Density is nearly frozen over decades (Fact 2).
  • Reconciliation:
    • History. Saxton & Benson’s existing density, and Marquis et al.’s Northeast and 1905 business community, are the same persistence the endowment captures. The Northeast’s larger sector is the regional gap in Fact 5. Models without history attribute part of the inherited level to whatever covariates correlate with it (D8). That includes region-linked ones such as diversity and income.
    • Size. The apparent density advantage of small counties is partly a feature of county units and per-capita measures (D2, D4).

Is consensus emerging?

Read through the multiverse, the literature agrees more than its signs suggest:

Covariate Consensus Condition
Education and wealth Positive Holds across designs
Poverty Negative across places Positive only within markets or over time (D2, D3)
Diversity Positive for grassroots; weak or negative for filers Name the population (D1) and the measure (D7); never use a count of groups (D4, D6)
Income inequality Modestly positive Holds with median income and poverty controlled
Civic participation Positive, mainly grassroots Use participation, not association counts
Government Funding complements; direct provision substitutes within places Causal direction unresolved; money received is partly an outcome
Size Population must be in the model A raw count without population measures city size (D4, D6)
History Strong persistence Control for it when the question is current conditions (D8)
Corporate presence Positive for large nonprofits (one study) Not yet tested in the multiverse; strongest for elite-oriented organizations where upper-class clubs and the Northeast’s traditions are present

What is not settled is causality. Every study here, and the multiverse itself, describes associations. The next step is designs that use changes a community or government can make (Part 10).

What it means for funders and governments. A local foundation reading this literature should:

  • give weight to findings that are stable across designs (education, civic participation, history);
  • treat diversity and poverty results as specific to the population and the question asked;
  • not read government-grant associations as evidence that public money builds sectors. They are consistent with that, but cannot show it.

Beyond the ten: recent and related studies

The ten studies above end in 2018. Since then the field has moved in three directions:

  • Density and growth studies now use national data, panels and spatial models.
  • Entry and exit: a separate literature studies which places gain and lose organizations.
  • Location within places: spatial analyses ask where nonprofits sit, and whether that matches need.

The tables below summarize 26 such studies. Unlike the ten, they are not compared coefficient by coefficient. Their outcomes, units and methods differ too much from ours for that. Instead, the discussion that follows asks whether each strand strengthens the claims of this page and the multiverse, or competes with them.

Verification. Every citation was checked against Crossref. - Read in full, or in large part: studies marked full. - Abstract only: the rest. Their findings come from the abstracts, and their data details are sometimes incomplete.

Two studies from England and Wales are included because they follow density over 50 years, which no US study does.

Do they strengthen our claims, or compete with them?

Each claim below comes from this page or from the multiverse.

Across places, poverty goes with fewer nonprofits; within places, organizations follow need

Strengthens, strongly. This is the claim the newer work supports most directly.

  • The same split, observed directly. McDonnell et al. (2020) find, in 40 years of data from England and Wales, that density is highest in the least deprived areas, yet rises within an area as it becomes more deprived. That is the across-versus- within distinction (D3) shown in one study.
  • Following need within a metro. Antipoverty nonprofits locate in higher-need tracts within a metro (Peck, 2008; Yan et al., 2014). That is the county-within-metro pattern of D2, at finer grain.
  • Fewer resources where need is high, across places:
    • rural and high-poverty counties have the thinnest safety nets (Shapiro, 2021);
    • high-poverty suburbs have weaker and more volatile ones (Allard & Pelletier, 2023).

Qualifies. The relationship need not be linear: - Wo (2018) find a U-shape across Los Angeles tracts. - McDonnell et al. (2026) find middle-deprivation areas densest.

The multiverse models poverty linearly, so a curved relationship would be absorbed into its average. That is worth testing.

Size has to be handled, and comparisons belong at the metro level

Strengthens. - Counts track population. Every recent density study that reports the correlation finds raw counts track population almost perfectly, r = .98 in Van Puyvelde & Brown (2016). All of them adjust for population, either per capita or as a control. - Corbin’s raw-count design has not been repeated. The field has converged on the D4 lesson.

Competes, in part: spatial spillovers. - M. K. Kim et al. (2026) and Never & Westberg (2016) find strong spillovers between neighboring counties. One county’s conditions shape its neighbors’ nonprofits, and neighbors free-ride on human-services spending. - That supports metro units (D2), since a metro brings commuting neighbors into one observation. - It also exposes an untested assumption: the multiverse treats metros as independent. Spillovers between metros are probably weaker than between counties, but a spatial-error check is a reasonable addition (Part 10).

Diversity works differently for different organizations

Strengthens the central point: name the population and the measure. - The meta-analysis. Lu (2020) find a positive but small heterogeneity effect across 37 studies. That is what the multiverse predicts when studies pool populations whose signs differ. - New, small, identity-based organizations rise with diversity: - registered arts, health and human-services charities rise with racial diversity (Jeong & Cui, 2020); - ethnic, cultural and folk organizations rise with immigration (Kim et al., 2022).

These are grassroots-like populations, where the multiverse finds diversity positive. - Established resources fall with diversity: - human-services spending is lower where the Black share is higher (Allard & Pelletier, 2023; Shapiro, 2026); - community foundations form in homogeneous counties (Wu, 2021).

These are the filer and spending populations, where the multiverse finds % nonwhite negative.

Adds a mechanism. The exit studies suggest why the two populations differ: - diverse counties have more dissolutions (Shon & Lu, 2024); - organizations in Black and Latino neighborhoods disband more often (Garrow, 2015; W. Lee, 2017).

High turnover keeps organizations small and unregistered as filers. That is consistent with more grassroots and fewer established organizations in diverse places, but not proof of it.

Competes. M. K. Kim et al. (2026) find heterogeneity negative for environmental nonprofits. That fits the subsector caution (D11): diversity findings are subsector-specific until shown otherwise. The multiverse models the full sector, so its grassroots-positive result may not hold in every field.

Government: funding complements, provision substitutes within places

Partly strengthens, partly competes.

  • Funding complements. Supported again:
    • M. K. Kim et al. (2026) find government grants and spending positive for environmental nonprofits;
    • a meta-analysis of 30 studies finds no crowding out (Lu & Xu, 2018).
  • Provision substitutes. This side is contested. Jeong & Cui (2020) find government size (government wages) negative in a cross-section of counties.
    • Our page reads that as a within-place effect.
    • The multiverse finds direct government slightly positive across metros.
    • Lu & Xu (2018) report that effect sizes depend on data structure and unit of analysis, which is what the identification argument predicts.
    A cross-sectional negative at the county level does not fit cleanly. It may reflect county units, where government payroll concentrates in county seats and capitals, or a real difference in the claim.
  • Verdict. “Funding complements” is now well supported. “Provision substitutes within places” is supported by within-place designs and contested by one cross-sectional one. It should stay a qualified claim.

Density is persistent, and history matters

Strengthens, strongly. - Persistent levels. Over 50 years, area densities follow the same trajectory while level differences persist (McDonnell et al., 2026). That is Fact 2, seen over a longer span than US data allow. - A mechanism for persistence. Dissolutions predict new foundings (Mayer, 2025), so organizations that close tend to be replaced. - Persistent leadership gaps. Deprived areas lose charities when volunteer leaders are not replaced (Clifford, 2024). That shows how gaps can persist.

Together these support controlling for history (D8), and the realistic time horizons in Part 10.

Resources, education and civic life

Strengthens. - Education. Positive in every model of Van Puyvelde & Brown (2016), and linked to conservation nonprofits (Willse, 2023). - Religious adherence. Positive in Jeong & Cui (2020). - Congregation density. Not significant in Van Puyvelde & Brown (2016). That is consistent with our reading of Corbin, where congregation measures that grow with size look positive only when size is not handled.

Competes, mildly: the older population. It is positive in Van Puyvelde & Brown (2016) and in Kim (2015), but weak in the multiverse: % 65+ has a median r of .02 for filers. The difference may be the unit: counties, including many small rural ones, versus metros.

What the new work adds that the multiverse does not cover

  • Rural places. Rural counties outside metro and micro areas fall outside the multiverse’s units. They are where safety nets are thinnest (Shapiro, 2021). A funder in a rural county should not assume the metro results apply.
  • Turnover. The multiverse models stocks. Entry and exit studies show that places with similar stocks can have very different churn. For local capacity building, churn matters as much as the level.
  • Who is served. Location studies show that where an organization is based is not where it serves (Marwell et al., 2026). Density by headquarters can overstate access in poor neighborhoods.

Overall. The newer literature mostly strengthens the page’s claims: - across places versus within places; - size handled explicitly; - diversity depends on the population; - history and persistence.

It competes on two points: whether direct government substitutes for nonprofits across counties, and the role of the older population. And it identifies three gaps: - spatial spillovers between metros; - rural counties; - turnover.

All three are now listed in Part 10.
