10. Where the research goes from here

Toward a framework for local foundations and governments

Note

Key points for discussion, 2026-11-18. To be developed into a full section.

The goal of this work is practical. It aims to give local foundations and governments that want to strengthen their nonprofit sectors a defensible way to:

  • benchmark the sector against comparable places;
  • understand which conditions are associated with a stronger sector;
  • judge what their own investments can and cannot be expected to change.

The database and multiverse are the foundation. This page lists what is still open and what new data, methods and analyses would move the work toward that goal.

What the work already offers a community

  • A fair benchmark. A place’s density relative to its size-expected level (its SAMI) compares like with like, so a small town is not judged against a large city (Bettencourt et al., 2010). Counts scale linearly with population but spending does not, so per-capita spending is a misleading yardstick.
  • Realistic time horizons. Density is nearly frozen over decades. After population growth, the sector catches up slowly: half the gap closes in about 5 years for filers and 9 for grassroots. A five-year grant cycle should not expect to move the level.
  • Robust versus fragile levers.
    • Some associations are positive in most designs. Education is the most robust, followed by foundation assets and government money that flows through Medicaid and public payroll.
    • Others flip sign with the design. A community should not build strategy on the fragile ones (Part 8, Findings).
  • Two sectors, not one. Established filers follow the local economy. Grassroots organizations follow endowment and demography. Each responds to different conditions, and investments may reach one and not the other.

Open questions

  1. What is causal? Nothing here is causal yet. The associations describe where strong sectors are, not what would make a sector stronger. This is the main gap for funders and governments, who need to know the effect of an action.
  2. Why the regional gap? The Northeast has more than twice the South’s nonprofit employment per resident, with similar for-profit employment. The leading explanation is institutional: who owns hospitals, and how states contract for services (Salamon, 1987). It has not been tested.
  3. How do grassroots organizations become established ones? Filer catch-up comes from existing organizations crossing the filing floor, not from new registrations. The pipeline from registration to a sustained, filing organization is the stage most open to local capacity-building, and it is the least understood.
  4. Do shocks reset the sector? The metros that rose fastest include several hit by disasters (New Orleans, Gulfport, Santa Rosa, Chico, Redding). Whether disaster recovery builds lasting capacity is still a hypothesis.
  5. Does giving respond to tax policy locally? Itemized contributions break at the 2018 tax change (the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act), so individual giving’s role is unresolved until that break is modelled.
  6. Size versus number. Is a community better served by more organizations or by larger ones? Counts and spending answer differently, and the right target depends on the community’s goal.

New data

  • Government funding received by nonprofits. The Form 990 government-grants line is in the e-file data from 2020. It was missing from the 2011–2019 extracts, so a consistent series has to be built from the e-file years forward. That would measure the delivery channel directly, rather than through Medicaid and public payroll.
  • Hospital ownership and state contracting. The American Hospital Association survey, CMS Provider of Services files and Medicaid managed-care contracts would test the institutional explanation of the regional gap.
  • Small organizations. Form 990-N (e-Postcard) filings and Form 1023-EZ approvals show which grassroots organizations are active and when new ones form. They would give a direct view of the registration-to-filer pipeline.
  • Local investments. Foundation grants by recipient location (990-PF Schedule and 990 Schedule I grants), plus community-foundation and local-government capacity-building programs. These are the “treatments” a causal design needs.
  • Nonprofit employment after 2022. The BLS research series ends in 2022, with no update announced before 2029. QCEW ownership codes or state unemployment-insurance records could extend it.
  • Corporate and elite presence. Public-company headquarters grow large nonprofits, especially elite-oriented ones (Marquis et al., 2013). The multiverse has no measure of corporate presence yet. Headquarters counts, from SEC filings, would test whether local business leadership is a lever for community funders.

New methods

  • Designs for causal effects:
    • event studies around shocks (disasters, plant closures, hospital conversions that create health-conversion foundations);
    • synthetic control for single communities that launched a sector-building initiative;
    • difference-in-differences across staggered state policy changes.
  • Stock-flow models. Model entry, exit and growth across the filing floor separately, so that a community can see which flow drives its gap. Recent work shows that dissolutions predict new foundings, and that turnover is higher in diverse and deprived places (Clifford, 2024; Mayer, 2025; Shon & Lu, 2024). Places with the same stock can differ greatly in churn.
  • Spatial dependence. County studies find strong spillovers between neighbors (Kim et al., 2026; Never & Westberg, 2016). Add a spatial-error check across metros to the multiverse, and test nonlinear need effects, which tract studies find U-shaped (Wo, 2018).
  • Small-area estimation and rural places. Most counties are small, and their densities are noisy. Rural counties outside metro and micro areas are outside the multiverse’s units, yet their safety nets are thinnest (Shapiro, 2021). Multilevel models with partial pooling would give stable benchmarks for rural places, where funders often have the least information.
  • Multiverse for causal designs. The decision framework applies equally to causal estimates. Treatment timing, comparison group and outcome window are choices that should be crossed and reported (Steegen et al., 2016; Young & Holsteen, 2017).

Limits of current designs, and how to fix them

Three problems run through the density literature, including its best within-place studies. Each has a known fix.

Panel models cannot identify slow-moving variables

The problem. Within-place panels compare each place with itself over time. For variables that barely change within a place, that leaves almost nothing to estimate from:

  • racial composition;
  • education;
  • religious structure;
  • regional culture.

In the multiverse, a within-metro estimator discards the 95–99% of variance that lies between metros (D3). What remains is mostly:

  • interpolation: ACS five-year averages and values carried between census years;
  • data breaks: splices between data sources;
  • short-run adjustment that lags population change.

In hybrid models, within effects are .03 SD or less for filers and spending, against .13–.42 SD between metros. A null or negative within-place estimate for race or education is therefore not evidence of no effect. It is evidence that the design cannot see one.

Three further problems compound this:

  • Measurement error. Interpolated covariates move smoothly between real observations, which biases within estimates toward zero.
  • Timing. Composition affects density over decades. A one-year or five-year within-place change cannot capture an effect that takes a generation to build.
  • Reverse causation. Nonprofit infrastructure can attract residents, as immigrant service organizations do, so composition and density shape each other.

Is there a study that does this well? Within the density literature, no. The six within-place studies either drop slow-moving variables or estimate them on little variation. Adjacent fields offer designs that work:

  • Diversity and public goods. The canonical cross-city study of diversity and public goods is cross-sectional (Alesina et al., 1999). Its strength is theory and measurement, not identification.
  • Education. Education’s local effects have been identified with historical instruments: the location of land-grant colleges as a source of variation in a city’s college share (Moretti, 2004).
  • Immigration and composition. Changes in local composition from immigration have been identified with past settlement patterns, which predict where new immigrants go, regardless of current local conditions (Card, 2001). These shift-share instruments now have a well-developed theory of when they are valid (Goldsmith-Pinkham et al., 2020).

Better specifications for slow-moving variables:

Specification What it identifies When to use it
Hybrid (within-between, correlated random effects) Between-place and within-place effects in one model, with the place means of covariates entered alongside their deviations (Bell & Jones, 2015; Mundlak, 1978) Default for panels: reports the long-run (between) and short-run (within) association together instead of discarding one
Long differences Change over 10–20 years regressed on covariate change, controlling for the starting level Composition and education, which change meaningfully only over decades
Shift-share or historical instruments Variation in composition or education driven by past settlement or historical institutions Causal claims about diversity or education
Event studies around discrete shocks The response to a sudden change in composition or need: refugee resettlement, plant closures, disasters Short-run causal effects with a clear timing
Cohort or entry models Whether new organizations form where composition is changing Separating today’s conditions from the inherited stock

The multiverse already includes cross-section, between, within and long-difference estimators, and hybrid models as evidence (D3). The literature should treat these as answers to different questions, not as more and less robust versions of one answer.

The literature ignores carrying capacity and path dependence

The problem. No study in the literature models density as a stock that adjusts, slowly, toward a level set by a place’s size and history. Every model treats the current density as an equilibrium. Two facts from the multiverse show why that misleads.

Carrying capacity. Density lags population (Fact 3).

  • Within a metro: after growth, half of the size-expected organizations are missing at first. The gap closes with a half-life of about 5 years for filers and 9 for grassroots.
  • Across metros: the Sunbelt deficit, about 25% for the fastest-growing fifth, closes with a half-life of about 35 years. Stock-flow accounting shows it is a lag, not a trait: fast growers add at least as many entrants per resident.
  • Decline is asymmetric. Organizations stay at first, then grassroots organizations shed the excess.

A model of density levels that ignores this reads every fast-growing place as “under-served” and every shrinking one as “over-served”. Covariates that go with growth then absorb the lag: in-migration, diversity, Sunbelt location. Ecological theories of organizations have described this kind of dynamic for decades (Hannan & Freeman, 1977), but the density literature has not used them.

Path dependence in market share. The nonprofit share of local employment varies widely and persistently (Fact 5).

  • Regional gap. The Northeast has 61 nonprofit jobs per 1,000 residents and the South 25, with similar for-profit employment.
  • Age of the sector. Older sectors hold larger shares.
  • History. The pre-1970 endowment, a sector’s inherited size, is one of the strongest covariates in the multiverse.

Much of today’s density is the residue of decisions made generations ago: who built the hospitals, universities and charities, and whether government contracted with them. Studies that leave history out attribute that residue to current income, diversity or politics.

How the literature can proceed:

  1. Model adjustment, not just levels. Use error-correction or partial-adjustment models: density moves toward a size-expected level at an estimated speed. Or enter a distributed lag of past population change, the growth kernels of the multiverse (D8).
  2. Separate growth from decline. Estimate the response to population gains and losses separately, since the adjustment is asymmetric.
  3. Control for history when the question is current conditions: the sector’s inherited size (the pre-1970 endowment), its age, and long-run growth before the study period. Report results with and without it, since history is also partly the outcome’s own past (D8).
  4. Treat market share as a benchmark or an outcome, not a control. It includes the hospitals and universities whose history explains it, and it rises with density itself.
  5. Link stocks and flows. Model entry, survival and crossing the filing floor together, so that a gap can be traced to too few foundings, too many exits, or a lag that will close on its own.

Most studies do not account for scaling

The problem. Larger places have more of almost everything, but not always in proportion.

  • Counts of filers scale with population at about 1.0 (Fact 1).
  • Spending scales faster: about 1.27 across metros and 1.46 across counties.
  • Size-scaled covariates correlate .80–.94 with log population (Fact 6): total income, government spending, foundation assets, numbers of congregations or corporations.

Two errors follow when a model ignores this:

  • Population-related bias. Per-capita ratios impose an exponent of 1. Where the true exponent differs, density per capita still rises or falls with size, and every covariate correlated with size absorbs that relationship. This is the classic problem of ratio variables (Kronmal, 1993; Uslaner, 1976).
  • Size-carrying covariates. Totals, or logged totals entered without log population, measure city size as much as the construct they are meant to capture.

How many studies fail to account for it? Of the 26 place-level studies in Part 9, none confirms estimating the scaling exponent. Of the 19 whose functional form we could confirm:

How population is handled Studies
Per capita, or a population offset (imposes an exponent of 1) 13
Raw counts, with population linear or absent 5
Logged outcome without log population 1
Log-log, or SAMI (exponent estimated) 0

The remaining seven could not be confirmed. Two may use log population as a control, but their papers do not make the form clear. All six within-place studies impose an exponent of 1 or enter population linearly.

In panels the problem does not go away. When a place grows, its per-capita density falls mechanically while the sector catches up (Fact 3). Any covariate that changes with growth picks up that lag.

The fix: the SAMI specification. This project introduces scale-adjusted metropolitan indicators to nonprofit density research. SAMIs come from urban scaling (Bettencourt et al., 2010). The specification has four parts:

  1. Estimate scaling instead of assuming it.
    • Regress log density on log population, year by year: \(\log Y_{it} = \alpha_t + \beta_t \log P_{it}\).
    • Take the residual as the SAMI: how far a place sits above or below the level expected for its size.
    • Equivalently, keep log population on the right-hand side of the model, so its exponent is estimated rather than fixed at 1 (D4).
  2. Treat size-scaled covariates the same way. Enter totals in SAMI form, or as logged totals with log population on the right. Rates such as poverty, % with a BA and turnout enter as they are (D6).
  3. For counts with zeros, use Poisson regression with log population as a regressor, not an offset, which re-imposes an exponent of 1. Never use log(1 + x) (D5).
  4. In panels, use year-specific SAMIs with place and year fixed effects, so the scaling relationship can shift over time.

What the fix changes. In the multiverse, the size-handling decisions change 13–32% of covariate verdicts:

  • predictor form changes the most;
  • then DV form;
  • then whether log population is on the right.

They are avoidable errors, not matters of judgment. A study that reports per-capita density with untransformed totals on the right is not making a different choice; it is making a fixable mistake.

Analyses that would serve funders and governments

  1. A community benchmark tool. Built on this database, it would show:
    • each county’s and metro’s filer, grassroots and spending SAMIs, and their trajectories since 2000;
    • its position among peer places matched on size, region and growth history;
    • its subsector mix against need (for example, human-services density relative to poverty).
  2. Gap diagnosis. Decompose a community’s gap into history (the pre-1970 endowment), recent growth (the catch-up lag) and current conditions. Only the last can be acted on.
  3. Capacity indicators. Track the share of registered organizations that file, the rate of crossing the filing floor, and survival after formation. These are leading indicators that move faster than density itself.
  4. Evaluation guidance. Translate the decision cards into a checklist for funders commissioning research:
    • which design choices change the answer;
    • which only look like robustness checks;
    • what time horizon an evaluation needs.
