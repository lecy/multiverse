0. Motivation

Why measure nonprofit density, and why do it as a multiverse

The question communities ask

Local foundations, community foundations, United Ways and city and county governments spend heavily on their nonprofit sectors. They fund organizations directly, contract with them for services, and invest in capacity-building, leadership programs and shared infrastructure. Behind that spending sit three practical questions:

  1. Is our nonprofit sector large or small for a place like ours?
  2. Which local conditions go with a stronger sector, and which of them can we change?
  3. How long should it take for an investment to show up in the sector’s size?

The research literature should help answer them, but in practice it gives a funder little to act on.

Why the evidence has not been usable

Nonprofit density has been studied for three decades. The theories are well developed:

  • Government failure: nonprofits supply public goods where preferences are diverse (Weisbrod, 1977).
  • Contract failure: nonprofits are trusted where quality is hard to judge (Hansmann, 1980).
  • Interdependence: government and nonprofits act as partners (Salamon, 1987).

The empirical tests disagree. Poverty, diversity, income and government spending have each been reported as positive in some studies and negative in others. And most studies compare places at one point in time. They describe where nonprofits are, but cannot say what changes a sector’s size. Only a handful follow places over time with within-place designs.

A funder reading this literature cannot tell whether the disagreements describe real differences between communities, or the many design choices each study made:

  • which organizations to count;
  • how to adjust for population;
  • whether to compare places or changes over time;
  • which unit and estimator to use.

Every one of those choices is defensible, and together they form a “garden of forking paths” (Gelman & Loken, 2014). A single study reports one path through it.

Two further problems make simple comparisons misleading:

  • Size. Bigger places have more of nearly everything. Counts of nonprofits grow in proportion to population, but spending does not (Bettencourt et al., 2007). Comparing per-capita spending across places of different size therefore ranks them by size.
  • History. A community’s nonprofit sector today largely reflects its past. Density is nearly frozen over decades. Without accounting for history, the effect of inherited size gets attributed to whatever current conditions correlate with it.

What this project does

The project addresses these problems in three steps:

  1. A national database (Parts 2–4). It counts every 501(c)(3) public charity in every US county and metro area, 2000–2024, and joins them to about 300 covariates. Every input is frozen so that the database can be rebuilt exactly.
  2. A fair benchmark (Parts 5 and 7). Each place is compared with its size-expected level, using scale-adjusted metropolitan indicators (Bettencourt et al., 2010). Seven stylized facts describe what any analysis of the data has to confront.
  3. A multiverse (Parts 6, 8 and 9). Rather than choosing one design, the analysis fits every defensible combination of choices: about 432,000 specifications (Steegen et al., 2016; Young & Holsteen, 2017).
    • It reports which conclusions hold under all of them, and which decisions change the answer.
    • It then reads ten well-known studies through those results, to show why they disagree and where they agree.

What a community can take from it

  • A benchmark that compares like with like: density relative to places of the same size, not raw counts per resident.
  • A list of robust and fragile associations.
    • Robust, positive under nearly every design: education, civic participation and the sector’s history.
    • Fragile: the result depends on which organizations are counted and which question is asked, as with diversity and poverty.
  • Realistic expectations: density changes slowly, so sector-building goals and evaluations need long horizons.
  • A research agenda (Part 10): the data, methods and analyses needed to move from association to evidence about what local action can change.
