1. Literature review

Nonprofit density, urban scaling and SAMIs, and multiverse analysis

This project draws on three bodies of work. The first explains why some communities have more nonprofits than others. The second, urban scaling, sets the yardstick: how many nonprofits a place of a given size should be expected to have. The third, multiverse analysis, makes visible how much a conclusion depends on the analyst’s choices. Together they answer a practical question for local foundations and governments: compared with places like it, is our nonprofit sector large or small, and which of the levers we control are actually associated with a stronger sector?

Nonprofit density

Why nonprofits exist. Economic theories explain the nonprofit sector by the limits of markets and governments:

  • Government failure. Government supplies public goods at the level the median voter wants. Where preferences are diverse, nonprofits supply the unmet demand (Weisbrod, 1977).
  • Contract failure. Where donors or clients cannot judge quality, the nondistribution constraint makes nonprofits more trustworthy providers than firms (Hansmann, 1980).
  • Interdependence. Voluntary action has its own failures: it is insufficient, particularistic, paternalistic and amateur. Government and nonprofits therefore act as partners, with public money flowing through nonprofit providers (Salamon, 1987).

Each theory implies a different set of local conditions associated with a larger sector: demand heterogeneity, information problems, or government spending.

Empirical tests across places. A line of studies tests these theories by comparing nonprofit counts or spending across states, metros and counties:

  • the joint size of the for-profit, nonprofit and government sectors (Ben-Ner & Van Hoomissen, 1992);
  • the growth of social-service nonprofits (Corbin, 1999);
  • metropolitan nonprofit sectors in NCCS data (Bielefeld, 2000);
  • community variations in the size and scope of the sector (Grønbjerg & Paarlberg, 2001);
  • a state panel test of government-failure theory (Matsunaga & Yamauchi, 2004);
  • social capital and sector growth (Saxton & Benson, 2005);
  • socioeconomic diversity and political engagement across counties (Kim, 2015);
  • government support and density in a metro panel (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013).

Inconsistent results. The covariates in these studies are much the same: education, income, poverty, diversity, philanthropy, government, civic culture. Yet their signs often disagree from study to study. Poverty and income, for example, change sign between pooled and fixed-effects models of the same metros (Lecy & Van Slyke, 2013).

Studies also differ in many design choices:

  • whether they count organizations or dollars;
  • whether they scale by population, and how;
  • the unit of analysis;
  • the years covered;
  • the estimator.

The disagreements could therefore come from the communities or from those choices, and the literature has had no way to tell which. That gap motivates the rest of this project.

Urban scaling and SAMIs

Scaling laws. Many urban quantities grow as a power of population, \(Y = Y_0 N^{\beta}\) (Bettencourt et al., 2007; West, 2017):

  • Superlinear (\(\beta > 1\)): innovation, wages and GDP grow faster than population.
  • Sublinear (\(\beta < 1\)): infrastructure grows more slowly.
  • Linear (\(\beta \approx 1\)): individual needs, such as housing and jobs, grow in proportion to population.

The exponents are explained by the way social interactions grow with city size (Bettencourt, 2013) and are linked to a production-function view of cities (Lobo et al., 2013).

SAMIs. If density were judged by raw counts per capita, the result would depend on whether the outcome scales linearly. Scale-adjusted metropolitan indicators (SAMIs) solve this: each city’s deviation from the scaling line, the log of observed over size-expected (Bettencourt et al., 2010).

  • Independent of size. SAMIs remove size from the comparison by construction.
  • Persistent. A city’s SAMI changes slowly over decades.
  • Meaningful. They separate local performance from the effect of size.

Per-capita ratios have a known flaw. They are biased whenever the numerator does not scale one-for-one with the denominator, and they can induce spurious correlations between ratios that share it (Uslaner, 1976).

Cautions.

  • Estimated exponents are sensitive to the definition of the unit and the estimator, and apparent nonlinearity has to be tested, not assumed (Leitão et al., 2016).
  • Cross-sectional exponents need not describe how any one city changes over time (Bettencourt et al., 2020; Depersin & Barthelemy, 2018).

Both cautions recur in our design: the unit of analysis, the estimator, and cross-sectional versus within-city identification are decisions in the multiverse (Part 6, D2–D5).

What it means for nonprofit density. Counts of 501(c)(3) organizations scale almost exactly linearly with population at every unit of aggregation, but spending does not (Part 7, Fact 1). A metro’s filer SAMI is therefore a size-neutral measure: how many nonprofits it has relative to a typical place of its size. That gives local funders and governments a fair benchmark, comparing like with like rather than a small town with a large city.

Multiverse analysis

The problem. Every estimate depends on a chain of defensible choices. Leamer (1983) argued for reporting how estimates move across reasonable specifications. Later work showed how undisclosed flexibility produces false positives (Simmons et al., 2011), and that even analysts who never search for results walk a “garden of forking paths” (Gelman & Loken, 2014).

The method.

  • Multiverse analysis fits every combination of defensible choices and reports the whole set (Steegen et al., 2016).
  • Specification curve analysis adds inference across the set (Simonsohn et al., 2020).
  • Multimodel analysis attributes the spread of an estimate to the choices that produce it (Young & Holsteen, 2017).
  • Which choices to fork. Not every choice belongs in one distribution. Choices that change the question (non-equivalent) should be separated from arbitrary ones (Del Giudice & Gangestad, 2021).

Evidence that it matters.

  • When many teams analyse the same data to answer the same question, their estimates vary widely: in sign, size and significance (Silberzahn et al., 2018).
  • Much of that variation cannot be traced to any team’s identifiable choices (Breznau et al., 2022).

Density research fits this description. It has standard covariates, many defensible designs and conflicting published signs.

How this project uses it.

  • Design. The multiverse separates non-equivalent decisions (what question is asked: filers or spending, metros or counties, levels or changes) from arbitrary ones (how a construct is measured, which estimator, how gaps are filled). Each decision has a card with its options and a recommendation (Part 6).
  • Execution. It fits about 432,000 specifications on one common yardstick (Part 8).
  • Reporting. It reports which decisions move results and which covariates hold up under all of them.

What this means for local funders and governments

The three literatures together suggest how a community can assess its nonprofit sector:

  1. Benchmark against size, not per capita. Compare a place with its size-expected level (its SAMI), not with raw counts per resident. Fact 1 shows counts scale linearly but spending does not, so per-capita spending comparisons mislead.
  2. Expect slow change. Density is persistent over decades (Fact 2), and growth leaves a lag that closes slowly (Fact 3). A sector’s size today largely reflects its history. Goals and evaluation horizons should match.
  3. Act only on robust associations. A factor that changes sign under reasonable alternative designs is not a reliable lever. The multiverse shows which local conditions are associated with a larger sector under every defensible design, and which are artefacts of a particular one.
  4. Distinguish filers from grassroots. Most registered charities never file a full return (Fact 4). They respond to different conditions from established filers, so policies aimed at one group may not reach the other.
